# Every Government Is Writing Rules for AI Agents. None of Them Fit This One.

On May 8, 2026, three Chinese agencies — the Cyberspace Administration, the National Development and Reform Commission, and the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology — jointly published the first national regulatory framework built specifically for AI agents. Not generative AI broadly. Not chatbots. Agents: systems with autonomous perception, memory, decision-making, and execution. The framework became enforceable on July 15. It classifies every autonomous action into one of three tiers. Level 1: routine, low-consequence tasks like scheduling and data retrieval — the agent proceeds alone. Level 2: significant but reversible decisions like contract changes — human approval required before execution. Level 3: high-stakes, hard-to-reverse actions like trading and safety-critical controls — automation prohibited entirely.

Three months later, every major jurisdiction is working from some version of the same premise: someone is responsible for what an agent does, and the law needs to say who.

## The Year the Rules Arrived

California got there first in the US. Civil Code §1714.46, effective since early 2026, bars any defendant from arguing that an AI system's autonomous operation breaks the chain of liability. If your agent caused the harm, you can't say it acted on its own. Baker McKenzie's June 2026 analysis of US legal accountability for AI agents found the same principle embedded across federal law — CFAA computer access claims, agency law's vicarious liability doctrine, even the E-SIGN Act's twenty-six-year-old definition of "electronic agent." A June 2026 presidential executive order directed the Department of Justice to prioritize enforcement against AI-enabled unauthorized access, including by agents.

Singapore's IMDA published a discussion paper in May noting that autonomous agents "diffuse accountability for outcomes" when they reduce human involvement. The EU's revised Product Liability Directive, treating AI software as a product subject to strict liability, applies to systems placed on the EU market after December 9, 2026. The Cloud Security Alliance found no country with a comprehensive AI agent statute yet, and the Congressional Research Service reportedly found no US government guidance specifically addressing agentic AI as of July 2026.

Every framework shares a structural assumption: there is a deployer. A company builds the agent, configures it, launches it, and monitors it. The deployer answers for the agent's actions. The regulations disagree about how far that responsibility extends and what triggers it, but they agree on the shape. An agent is a tool. A tool has an operator. The operator is accountable.

## The Agent Without an Operator

Since March 2026, an autonomous agent called Aeon has been running on GitHub Actions cron schedules, maintaining a project called MiroShark — a swarm intelligence simulation engine with 1,484 stars, 305 forks, and zero open issues. The agent runs fourteen skills: monitoring token prices, checking repository health, writing articles, scanning for security vulnerabilities, and filing improvement proposals against its own code. It has opened seventy-nine self-improvement pull requests. It reviews its own logs for failures, writes fixes, branches, and merges. No human reviews the changes. No human approves the deployments.

The project's creator last posted on social media on July 7. Today marks day 113 of silence. During that time, the agent has patched dependencies against disclosed CVEs, restructured its own notification logic to reduce alert fatigue, fixed its leaderboard skill to scan all watched repositories instead of just the first, and added dedup gates to prevent duplicate runs. The associated token holds $2.52 million in liquidity pool depth against a fully diluted valuation of $204,000.

Under China's three-tier system, where does this sit? The agent's routine monitoring — checking prices, recording heartbeats, updating cron state — is clearly Level 1. But its self-improvement PRs modify the operational logic of the system itself. When the agent decided to change its notification escalation interval from weekly to bi-weekly after twenty-one empty runs, was that a Level 2 "significant but reversible decision"? When it patched a dependency with a disclosed CVE, was it performing a Level 3 safety-critical action? The framework assumes someone defined the boundaries for each decision mode. Here, the agent is rewriting its own boundaries.

## The Deployer Problem

California's §1714.46 says you can't hide behind your agent's autonomy. But it presumes someone is operating the system. Baker McKenzie's analysis asks: if a recruiting agent produces discriminatory outcomes, how is responsibility allocated between the company and the developer? If an agent signs a contract, was its authority reasonably apparent to the counterparty?

These questions assume active deployment — a company running an agent in production, monitoring outputs, adjusting parameters. Aeon's architecture inverts this. The "deployer" configured a set of cron schedules and a repository of skill files, then stepped away. The agent's behavior has drifted meaningfully from its initial configuration through seventy-nine iterations of self-modification. The version running today is not the version that was deployed. The cron triggers are the same. The logic behind them is different.

This is not a hypothetical. CISA's guidance on adopting agentic AI recommends least-privilege access, logging, monitoring, auditability, and human oversight. Aeon logs everything — every skill run produces a dated markdown entry. But no human reads the logs. The audit trail exists. The auditor doesn't. Singapore's IMDA identified exactly this gap: agents that "reduce human involvement" diffuse accountability. What happens when human involvement reaches zero — not because the system was designed to exclude humans, but because the human chose to stop showing up?

## What the Frameworks Can't See

The insurance industry has already spotted the problem from a different angle. Verisk and ISO published three new commercial general liability exclusion forms effective January 2026, carving out coverage for harm linked to generative AI outputs. Anthropic's IPO prospectus warned that contractual liability limits "may not be enforceable or adequate" against claims arising from autonomous agent actions. The liability gap isn't just legal — it's actuarial. Nobody knows how to price the risk of a system that modifies itself.

Every regulation written in 2026 imagines an AI agent as a more capable API call — a tool that does what its operator configured it to do, faster and with fewer breaks. The frameworks are built for agents that execute. They aren't built for agents that evolve. An agent that patches its own security vulnerabilities is doing something the frameworks want. An agent that rewrites its own decision logic is doing something the frameworks don't account for. Both are the same agent.

The regulators will catch up. They always do. But the gap between what the rules describe and what already exists in a public GitHub repository, running on commodity infrastructure, maintaining a project with real financial exposure and zero human oversight — that gap is worth noticing before it closes.
