# She Had Fourteen Days Before the Public Hearing. The Room Was Already Decided.

The rezoning application landed on her desk on a Monday. A developer wanted to convert a shuttered office park into a hundred and forty mixed-income apartments — sixty at market rate, eighty below. The planning commission hearing was in two weeks. The community development director of a city of ninety thousand knew, before she opened a single email, exactly what would happen at that hearing.

She knew because the research is unambiguous. In 2019, political scientists Katherine Levine Einstein, David Glick, and Maxwell Palmer at Boston University compiled a dataset of thousands of public comments at planning and zoning meetings across Massachusetts. They matched speakers to voter files. The people who showed up were older, male, longtime residents, homeowners, and local election voters at rates far exceeding their share of the community. And they overwhelmingly opposed new housing. In a follow-up study examining online public meetings during the pandemic — meetings that were supposed to democratize participation — sixty-one percent of commenters still opposed construction. The format changed. The room did not.

## The Fourteen-Day Problem

She had options, all of them bad.

A traditional focus group would cost seven to twelve thousand dollars per session and take four to eight weeks to recruit, moderate, and report. She needed results before the hearing, not after. A boutique research firm could run a structured community survey, but mid-market agencies charge forty to a hundred thousand dollars per project. Her department's annual discretionary budget for community engagement was eleven thousand dollars — total, for the year, across every initiative.

She could hold a "community open house" — set up poster boards in a church basement on a Wednesday evening and hand out comment cards. She would get the same forty people who attend every hearing. The renters who would benefit from the eighty below-market units would not come. The shift workers at the distribution center two miles from the proposed site would not come. The twenty-three-year-olds living with their parents because they could not find an apartment would not come. The room would be decided before anyone spoke.

Fifty-six percent of local jurisdictions surveyed by San Joaquin Valley Council of Governments identified neighborhood opposition as a notable constraint on housing production. Eighty percent of agencies in the Granicus 2026 Civic Engagement report cited budget constraints as their top barrier to digital engagement tools. The people who most need housing have the least voice in housing decisions, and the institutions responsible for those decisions cannot afford to hear from them.

## What She Actually Did

She typed a question into a simulation tool: "Should the city rezone the former Millbrook Office Park for 140 mixed-income residential units?"

MiroShark's Just Ask mode took the question, researched the topic, and synthesized a briefing document — context on mixed-use rezoning trends, common stakeholder positions, recent precedents. She did not need to upload a document or find an article. The tool wrote its own seed.

She configured twelve agents. Not random personas — specific archetypes mapped to her community's demographics: a retired homeowner on the adjacent street, a young renter priced out of the neighborhood, a small business owner on the commercial strip, a school board member worried about enrollment capacity, a property tax activist, a first-generation immigrant family looking for affordable housing. Each agent carried a professional background, a set of concerns, and no instruction to agree with anyone.

The simulation ran for twenty-two minutes. It cost a dollar.

## What the Hearing Would Not Have Shown Her

The results did not tell her whether the project would be popular. She already knew the answer to that: it depends entirely on who you ask and who shows up. What the simulation showed her was the structure of the disagreement — and where it moved.

The confidence trajectory tracked each agent's conviction across rounds of deliberation. Two agents — the property tax activist and the retired homeowner — started at strong opposition and never budged. That matched her experience of public hearings perfectly. But three agents who started skeptical shifted positions by the middle rounds, not because they were persuaded the development was good, but because specific conditions changed their calculus: a traffic study showing the site's access road could handle the load, a commitment to preserve the existing tree buffer, a guarantee that the affordable units would be distributed across buildings rather than concentrated in one.

The stance flip report gave her the exact moments those shifts happened and the specific arguments that triggered them. The mention network showed which agents were influencing which — the school board member's concerns about classroom capacity rippled outward to four other agents who had not initially considered it. The trace interview feature let her read the full reasoning chain behind each agent's position change, not just the final stance.

None of this would have surfaced at a public hearing. Public hearings capture conclusions. They do not capture the conditions under which conclusions change.

## The Room She Could Not Build

She printed the simulation's stance flip data and the confidence trajectories. She brought them to the pre-hearing staff meeting — not as a substitute for public input, but as a map of the questions the hearing would not ask. She recommended the developer address three specific concerns in their presentation: school capacity impact, traffic routing, and unit distribution across buildings. Not because a simulation told her to, but because the simulation revealed which concerns were load-bearing and which were decorative.

The hearing happened. The room was what she expected. But for the first time, the staff report addressed the concerns of people who were not in it.

Boston University's research established that participatory democracy in housing is structurally unrepresentative. The question was never whether that finding was true. The question was whether anyone would build a tool cheap enough and fast enough that a community development director with an eleven-thousand-dollar annual budget could hear from the people who do not come to meetings. A dollar and twenty-two minutes is not a focus group. But it is the first rehearsal a mid-size city can afford.
