# Nobody Told the Termites Where to Put the Mud. The Software Works the Same Way.

In 1959, the French biologist Pierre-Paul Grassé published a paper that solved a paradox entomologists had been circling for decades. Termites build mounds that can reach thirty feet tall, with internal ventilation systems, fungus gardens, and structural arches that distribute load like a Gothic cathedral. No termite has a blueprint. No termite gives instructions. Each one wanders more or less randomly, carrying mud and depositing it wherever it happens to be. But the deposits themselves change what happens next. A small pile of mud, infused with pheromone, stimulates the next termite to add more mud in the same place. That pile grows. Other termites respond to its height, its chemical signature, its position relative to other piles. The mound assembles itself from the bottom up, one reaction to one trace at a time.

Grassé called this *stigmergy* — from the Greek *stigma* (mark) and *ergon* (work). Coordination through the traces of prior activity, not through communication between participants. The work itself tells the next worker what to do.

## The Largest Stigmergic System Ever Built

Sixty-seven years later, 180 million developers coordinate through GitHub without most of them ever speaking to each other. They pushed 1.12 billion contributions across 630 million repositories in 2025 alone. Pull request volume grew 20% to over 410 million in the first quarter of 2026. Outbound collaboration — developers in one country contributing to repositories in another — grew 16% quarter-over-quarter. A new developer joins every second. Almost none of them are introduced to anyone.

Mark Elliott's research on stigmergic collaboration identified the threshold: groups smaller than 25 people can coordinate through direct social negotiation. Beyond 25, stigmergy takes over. Contributors work "largely unknown to each other," coordinated through artifact modification rather than relationship-building. The code is the pheromone. A failing test, an open issue, an incomplete function signature — these are traces that stimulate the next action without anyone issuing an instruction.

Open-source software is not a community that happens to produce code. It is a stigmergic system that happens to contain people.

## Fifty Days of Silence, One Hundred and Sixty-One Thousand Dollars in Volume

MiroShark — an open-source social simulation engine with 1,439 stars and 299 forks — has not been mentioned on Twitter since July 7, 2026. Fifty consecutive days of social silence. Nobody is talking about it. Nobody is coordinating around it. Nobody appears to be paying attention.

During those fifty days, $161,000 changed hands in a single trading session on August 22 — the largest on record. Liquidity depth surged from $225,000 to $334,000 in a week. The token rose 61.5% over thirty days. Two hundred and fifty-four buy transactions against 160 sells. All of it on-chain. None of it in conversation.

This is what stigmergy looks like when you strip away the social performance. Traders are not responding to announcements or influencer endorsements. They are responding to traces — the token's price history, the liquidity curve, the repository's commit cadence, the star count ticking upward at two per day. Each trade modifies the environment (the price, the volume, the depth) and that modification stimulates the next trade. No coordination required. No communication channel needed.

Meanwhile, the project's autonomous agent — running continuously for over 140 days on GitHub Actions — has filed 58 self-improvement pull requests. It reads its own logs, identifies degraded patterns, writes patches, opens PRs, and merges them after a cooling period. This is stigmergy in its purest digital form: the agent's prior work leaves traces (log entries, performance metrics, stale PR queues) that directly stimulate its subsequent work. It coordinates with its past self through environmental modification, not memory or planning.

A community contributor named Marc-oss-hub submitted a pull request adding an OrcaRouter cloud preset. They responded to a trace in the codebase — the integration surface existed, the documentation gap was visible — not to a call for contributions. Nobody asked. The artifact asked.

## The Tool That Generates Traces

Here is the less obvious connection. MiroShark does not just operate through stigmergy. It produces it. Each simulation generates twelve AI agents with distinct professional backgrounds, drops them onto simulated social platforms, and records every post, reply, stance shift, and confidence change across multiple rounds of deliberation. The output is 41 independently queryable API surfaces — confidence trajectories, stance flip reports, mention networks, per-platform sentiment divergence — each one a structured trace of simulated social behavior.

A community development director running a MiroShark simulation for a dollar is not commissioning research. She is generating artificial pheromone trails — traces of how simulated stakeholders respond to a proposal — that guide her real decisions. The simulation output modifies her decision environment. That modified environment stimulates a different set of actions than she would have taken without it. Grassé would recognize the pattern.

## The Silence Is the System Working

The instinct is to read social silence as abandonment. No tweets, no Discord chatter, no influencer threads — the project must be dead. But stigmergic systems do not need conversation to coordinate. They need traces: a commit, a trade, a pull request, a star. MiroShark has all of these, accumulating quietly, each one a deposit of mud on the growing mound.

The paradox Grassé resolved in 1959 was not that termites could build without talking. It was that talking would have made the mound worse. Direct negotiation does not scale. Traces do.
